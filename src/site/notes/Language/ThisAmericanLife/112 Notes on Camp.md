# 1 Prologue

**Ira Glass** It's a typical camp, all the normal activities—canoeing, archery, sports. There are two girls who everybody calls the *homesick*[^2] girls, who will cheerfully identify themselves that way to anybody they meet.  
  **Girl 1** She wants to go home.  
  **Homesick Girl 1** I do. I swear to God, I want to go home.  
  **Girl 2** The food is like the best.  
  **Homesick Girl 2** I want to go home too.  
  **Girl 3** Including me.  
**Ira Glass** Because it's a pretty upscale[^1] camp, there's also horseback riding. And on the rifle range, suburban girls from Chicago's North Shore lay on their bellies in sniper position while a real former Israeli soldier *barks orders* about how to shoot.  
  **Instructor** I know it's really hard for you ladies to keep it quiet, but try, OK? Aim and fire.  
  GUNSHOTS
**Ira Glass** The boys' cabins are all named after Indian tribes, the girls' cabins are all named after women's colleges. So perky little suburban kids are constantly informing you, I'm an Apache, I'm Mohawk, I'm in Vassar, I'm in Bryn Mawr. And it seems pretty simple, but everybody here will tell you, no one back home understands it. None of their friends, nobody. There is just a gap between camp people and non-camp people.  
  **Girl 1** I know. All the time during school, I'm like, I want to go to camp. I want to do this.  
  **Girl 2** Camp is all I talk about during the year.  
  **Man** Really?  
  **Girl 2** Yeah. My friends *get so mad at* me. They're like, we don't want to hear about camp anymore.  
  **Girl 2** Because everything that goes on at home, you can always think of something that relates something that happened at camp. And so then you tell that, and they'll be like, camp? We've heard about camp way too much[^5].  
  **Girl 1** It's also little stories you tell, and you think they're so funny and everybody in your cabin understands them. And then you go home and you're laughing, and they give you a look like, what are you talking about? Like, that's stupid.  
**Ira Glass** Well, today on our program, we try to bridge the gap between camp people and non-camp people who try to understand what is the cult-like, mystical[^3] connection some people feel with their summer camps? From WBEZ Chicago and Public Radio International, it's This American Life. I'm Ira Glass.  
**Ira Glass** Today on our program, Notes On Camp. Six different notes, some recorded at a summer camp, and some amazing stories that actually came from you, people who listen to this program and called us up to talk camp. Stay with us.  
**Ira Glass** Most of today's program takes place in Michigan, not far from Paw Paw. Two affiliated[^4] camps, Greenwoods and Lake of the Woods, one is a boys camp, one's a girls camp. But they sit on the same land. They share everything. Basically they function as one camp. Producer Julie Snyder and I spent a fair amount of time there.  
**Ira Glass** Julie usually hung out with the girls, I usually stayed with the boys. Here then, our findings.

# 2 Act Two: Fear

**Ira Glass** Note number two, Fear. Sure, kids today, so *sophisticated*[^6]. They have email and PlayStation. It is encouraging, then, to realize that if you *get them living in the woods*[^7] a few weeks, they can be entertained by some of the most *stupidly primitive forms of entertainment*[^8] available since the dawn of civilization. I'm talking about scary stories. Every camp has got them. This is so true you do not even need me to tell this to you. 12-year-olds know this.
  **Girl 1** You know how every camp has their little ghost story?
  **Sarah** Ours is about—
  **Girl 1** We have the story of Turtleman.
  **Sarah** I'll tell it.
  **Girl 1** OK. Sarah will tell it.
  **Boy** The Turtleman story? All right, I'll tell it.
  **Sarah** Long—you can help me out if I do it wrong. A long, long time ago, there was this counselor who was really mean to all of his campers and everything.
  **Boy** I can't remember how many years ago it was. But *supposedly*[^9], there was this counselor, and he was *dishonorably discharged from the military*[^10]. And he was real, real mean to all the campers. He always took their food *and stuff* and, like, stole their clothes and stuff like that.
  **Sarah** He would beat them and everything, and his campers were so afraid of him.
  **Girl 2** And he said, if you tell anybody about me—
  **Sarah** Yeah, he said, if you tell anyone about me, then I'll kill you all.
  **Girl 3** And he would read their letters, so they couldn't write home and tell about it.
  **Boy** And one year, these campers had a *foot locker*[^11] filled with fireworks. But he found them, and he took them from them.
  **Sarah** And so, on the Fourth of July, *the guys were like*[^12], oh, well, he's so mean. We should try and kill him ourselves. We're not really this *vicious*[^13] here, but—so they put fireworks under his bed. And once he had fallen asleep, they all went up there and they lit—
  **Girl 1** No, he was smoking.
  **Sarah** Oh.
  **Girl 1** He was smoking a cigarette.
  **Boy** And then I guess they could smoke in the cabins because, I mean, it was many, many years ago, and I guess there wasn't a law. And he took them, and he put the fireworks underneath his bed, and he fell asleep.
  **Girl 3** And, well, a cigarette fell out of his hand and into the foot locker.
  **Sarah** The fireworks got lit, and he exploded. And he ran into the lake on fire.
  **Boy** And supposedly, he's still alive.
  **Sarah** Every Fourth of July, he comes back, and he *takes revenge on*[^14] a camper and pulls him into the lake.
  **Boy 2** Every Fourth of July, he comes out of the lake and takes someone in for his next victim. But what really happens on the Fourth of July is either a counselor or one of the Mohawks, without asking anyone, goes into the lake and comes out and *scares the heck out of*[^15] everyone.
  **Boy 3** But yeah, some of them really believe it. So it's cool.
**Ira Glass** The older kids are funny in how they *handle*[^16] the ghost stories with the little kids. When producer Julie Snyder was interviewing a group of girls about Turtleman during arts and crafts one day, there was this moment. 12-year-olds Rebecca Stern and Sarah Turkisher were talking about how they used to believe in Turtleman a few years ago when they were little, when, suddenly, some of the younger girls sitting around, Jenny Mayer and Nicole Horn, *piped up*[^17], asking, well, is it true? And then the older girls, unsure of what to say, *in the course of*[^18] 17 seconds *take every possible position on the issue*[^19]. Here's the tape.
  **Sarah Turkisher** And then you tell all the little kids, but you're kind of cautious—
  **Jenny Mayer** It's not true, is it?
  **Sarah Turkisher** No.
  **Jenny Mayer** You said it was true.
  **Sarah Turkisher** It is true.
  **Jenny Mayer** Who made it up, though?
  **Rebecca Stern** It's a legend.
  **Sarah Turkisher** It's a legend.
  **Rebecca Stern** It's a camp legend.
  **Sarah Turkisher** There's always a legend of camp.
  **Rebecca Stern** Yeah.
  **Jenny Mayer** So is it true, or isn't it true?
  **Sarah Turkisher** It's not.
  **Rebecca Stern** You'll never know.
  **Sarah Turkisher** Time does not tell.
  **Rebecca Stern** No. You'll find out.
**Ira Glass** The 10-year-olds seem to sit *on the cusp of belief and disbelief*[^20] when it comes to stories like this. **For them, talking about these stories is just part of the process of trying to *puzzle out*[^21], how does the world exactly work?** When you're 10, remember, people are constantly telling you scary stories—of murders, of kids with guns—and the thing is, a lot of those stories are true. So you're still figuring out, where is the line between fiction and nonfiction?
**Ira Glass** One Saturday night, Aaron Etchler had all of his fellow 10-year-olds in the Sioux cabin completely *worked up*[^22] by telling them the story of Candyman. Candyman, if you know this story, appears and kills you if you say his name 10 times. Aaron said that a friend of his had died this way.
  **Boy 1** He said his friend died from Candyman.
  **Boy 2** He was crying last night because of the Candyman.
  **Boy 1** I was *this close to*[^23] killing myself because it was so scary.
  **Boy 2** And that *freaked me out*[^24], like hysterically.
  **Boy 1** We had to leave the bathroom light on last night because I keeped on saying, turn on the bathroom light on, man, I'm so scared.
  **Boy 2** We kept our light on the whole night because we were so scared.
**Ira Glass** What could be more fun than getting scared like that? Nearly all of them had trouble sleeping, so much trouble that they were not allowed to tell any scary stories Sunday night at *lights out*[^25]. So instead, they tried something else, an experiment. I joined them after dark, after evening activity, right before lights out, in their cabin.
  **Boy 1** Who's ready?
  **Boy 2** Let's do it.
  **Boy 3** OK, guys. Ready to be chantful[^26].
  **Boy 1** Everybody in.
  INTERPOSING VOICES
  **Boy 1** —Bloody Mary—
  **Alex** I'm Alex, and we're going to do a little seance[^27] over Bloody Mary. We're going to go into the bathroom. I think our whole cabin is going to because this is going to be a great time.
  **Boy 3** Yeah.
  **Alex** What you're going to probably see is a bloody face when you say Bloody Mary 40 times.
  **Boy 1** You'll see the bloody face of Bloody Mary.
  **Ira Glass** In the mirror?
  **Boy 1** Yes. Face in the blood, very ugly.
  **Boy 3** Bloody face.
  **Boy 1** It's going to be scary.
**Ira Glass** I squeeze into the tiny bathroom with 12 10-year-old boys. Some of the boys hold their arms as if they're *cradling a baby*.
  **Boy 2** If you act like you have a baby in your hands, and you say Bloody Mary 50 times, then you'll have, actually, a dead baby in your hands.
  **Ira Glass** And why a baby?
  **Boy 1** It's Bloody Mary's baby, they say. She threw it up in the air, and she didn't catch it in time, and it fell.
  **Ira Glass** That's the story?
  **Boy 1** Yeah.
  **Boy 2** It's called Blue Baby. It's called Blue—
  **Boy 3** Baby Blue. Not Blue Baby.
  **Boy 1** It's time to do this Bloody Mary bloody thing. Turn off the lights.
  **Boy 2** Let's get scared. Ready? 1, 2—
  **Boys** **(CHANTING)** Bloody Mary. Bloody Mary. Bloody Mary. Bloody Mary. Bloody—
**Ira Glass** They're 10. In a couple of years, there'll be cynics[^28] in the bunch, and they'll all be competing to prove they're too cool to believe in stuff like Bloody Mary. But for now, we all watch the mirror.
  **Boys** Bloody Mary. Bloody Mary. Bloody Mary. Bloody Mary.
  **Boy 1** Bloody Mary. Stop! Crap, it didn't—how many times did you do it?
  **Boy 2** 40 times.
  **Boy 3** You have to do it 50—
**Ira Glass** They're so ready to believe that when the face doesn't appear in the mirror, their first explanation is that maybe you need to chant 50 times, not 40. And then—
  **Boy 1** Guys, why did you do that?
  **Boy 3** I felt a baby in my hands.
  **Boy 1** He felt a baby in his hands. It worked.
  **Boy 3** I felt a baby in my hands.
  **Boy 4** So do I. And you're supposed to go like this.
  **Ira Glass** So did it work?
  **Boy 1** Yeah.
  **Boy 2** Yeah.
  **Boy 4** It worked in a different way than we expected it to, but you felt Baby Blue in your hands.
**Ira Glass** It took a while after that for them to settle into their beds. When I left half an hour later, it did not seem like anybody was going to be falling asleep very quickly.
